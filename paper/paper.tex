\Abstract{
Corpus linguistics, digital humanities, and computational social
science routinely require \emph{comparing} text corpora --- across
political alignments, across time, across discourse contexts. The
existing tooling is fragmented across the corpus-linguistics
ecosystem (\proglang{R}'s \pkg{quanteda}, the closed-source Sketch
Engine platform), visualisation-first packages
(\pkg{Scattertext}, \pkg{BERTopic}), and the \proglang{Python} NLP
stack (\pkg{nltk}, \pkg{spaCy}, \pkg{gensim},
\pkg{sentence-transformers}). No \proglang{Python} package has
unified the comparative workflow under a single coherent
application programming interface (API).

This paper presents \pkg{pycorpdiff}, a \proglang{Python} package
that provides the missing comparative layer. The package exposes
three public verbs --- \fct{compare(a, b)}, \fct{track(c, term)},
and \fct{compare.before\_after()} --- that consolidate keyness
analysis (Dunning log-likelihood with Hardie's LogRatio effect
size, Pearson $\chi^2$, dispersion sanity checks, and
Benjamini--Hochberg multiple-comparison correction), collocation
shift (window-based co-occurrence with logDice, pointwise mutual
information, $t$-score, and Daille's MI$^3$), temporal trajectories
with Wilson score confidence intervals, offline and online
changepoint detection, segmented-regression interrupted time
series, Bayesian structural time-series counterfactual analysis,
state-space exponential-smoothing forecasting, and embedding-based
semantic shift via averaged contextual representations with
Procrustes alignment. Every analytical result carries its evidence:
each result object exposes \fct{.explain(term)} which returns KWIC
concordance lines from the source corpora.

\pkg{pycorpdiff} is positioned as orchestration over the existing
ecosystem rather than reinvention. Tokenisers and embedders plug
in via two \class{typing.Protocol} extension points, with one-line
adapters for \pkg{spaCy}, \pkg{Stanza}, \pkg{jieba}, \pkg{fugashi},
and any \pkg{sentence-transformers}-compatible model. The base
install depends only on \pkg{numpy}, \pkg{pandas}, \pkg{scipy}, and
\pkg{pyarrow}; visualisation, semantic, temporal-statistics,
multilingual, and out-of-core functionality are opt-in extras.

We describe the package's three-layer architecture, the
statistical defaults that drive each analytical method, two
worked examples (a real-data analysis of the 2012 United States
presidential convention speeches and a temporal-stack
demonstration on a representative parliamentary corpus), and a
fully self-contained replication archive that regenerates every
figure and numeric table from the published code. Numerical
results have been cross-validated against five reference
implementations: \pkg{quanteda} (\proglang{R}), \pkg{NLTK},
\pkg{Scattertext}, Rayson's log-likelihood wizard, and the
HistWords diachronic word embeddings of \cite{hamilton2016}.
}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Researchers in corpus linguistics, digital humanities,
computational social science, communication studies, political
discourse analysis, and the digital history of ideas routinely
need to answer a small set of comparative questions. How does
corpus $A$ differ from corpus $B$ at the level of vocabulary, of
collocational behaviour, of meaning? How has the language used
around a particular term evolved across decades? Did a specific
event --- an election, a policy change, a market collapse ---
actually alter discourse in measurable ways, or did the
apparent shift merely continue a pre-existing trajectory? These
are old questions, and the methodological toolkit is mature.
Dunning's log-likelihood \citep{dunning1993} has anchored
keyness analysis for three decades; Church and Hanks's pointwise
mutual information \citep{church1991} and Rychl\'y's logDice
\citep{rychly2008} are standard collocation measures; Hardie's
LogRatio \citep{hardie2014logratio} and Wilson's Bayes-factor
approach \citep{wilson2013} provide effect-size companions to the
Dunning statistic; \cite{hamilton2016} established diachronic
word-embedding analysis as a standard tool for tracking semantic
change; \cite{brodersen2015causalimpact} introduced Bayesian
structural time-series modelling for counterfactual causal
inference; and \cite{adams2007bocpd} established Bayesian online
changepoint detection.

What has \emph{not} kept pace with the methodology is the
\proglang{Python} tooling. The corpus-linguistic and discourse-
analytic methods listed above live in at least four largely
non-interoperable ecosystems. The first, classical corpus
linguistics, is dominated by the Sketch Engine platform
\citep{kilgarriff2014sketchengine}, which is closed-source and
hosted, and by \pkg{quanteda} \citep{benoit2018quanteda} in
\proglang{R}, which is rigorous but inaccessible to the large
plurality of researchers whose data-analysis fluency is in
\proglang{Python}. The second, visualisation-first packages,
includes \pkg{Scattertext} \citep{kessler2017scattertext} and
\pkg{BERTopic} \citep{grootendorst2022bertopic}, which each
provide one valuable view but no comparative-analysis layer
beneath them. The third, modern \proglang{Python} NLP, includes
\pkg{nltk} \citep{bird2009nltk}, \pkg{spaCy}, \pkg{gensim}, and
\pkg{sentence-transformers} \citep{reimers2019sbert}, each
best-of-breed within its niche --- tokenisation, syntactic
analysis, topic modelling, sentence embeddings --- but none of
which provides a comparative API. The fourth, temporal and
time-series tooling, includes \pkg{statsmodels}
\citep{seabold2010statsmodels}, \pkg{ruptures}
\citep{truong2020ruptures}, and \pkg{sktime}; these are not
corpus-aware and require a substantial scaffolding to apply to
the rate-per-period series that diachronic corpus work produces.

The contribution of \pkg{pycorpdiff} is the missing comparative
layer. The package is deliberately not a general NLP framework:
it does not retokenise, reimplement word embeddings, or train
models. It orchestrates the existing ecosystem behind three
public verbs --- \fct{compare}, \fct{track}, and
\fct{compare.before\_after} --- and a small set of result classes
(\class{KeynessResult}, \class{CollocationShiftResult},
\class{SemanticShiftResult}, \class{TemporalTrajectory},
\class{NetworkResult}, \class{ForecastResult},
\class{CausalImpactResult}, \class{BocpdResult},
\class{ConcordanceResult}) that share a uniform six-method
contract. Every analytical claim is accompanied by KWIC evidence
on demand via \fct{Result.explain(term)}, so a researcher
ranking the top fifty Republican-leaning terms in convention
speeches can immediately retrieve the sentences from which the
ranking was derived.

This paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related}
surveys the related work that pycorpdiff builds on and the
existing tools it is positioned against. Section~\ref{sec:design}
describes the three-layer architecture and the
\class{typing.Protocol}-based extension points. Section~\ref{sec:api}
walks through the public API. Section~\ref{sec:example} presents
two worked examples: a real-data analysis of the 2012 United States
presidential convention speeches \citep{kessler2017scattertext} and
a temporal-stack demonstration on a representative parliamentary
fixture. Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks} compares pycorpdiff against
\pkg{NLTK} on shared workloads. Section~\ref{sec:defaults} surveys
the statistical defaults and the reasoning behind each.
Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility} covers testing and
cross-validation infrastructure. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}
concludes.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Corpus linguistics and keyness}

The methodological foundation of keyness analysis is Dunning's
log-likelihood ratio test \citep{dunning1993}, which corrects the
small-cell unreliability of Pearson's $\chi^2$ on the contingency
tables that corpus-comparison naturally produces. Dunning's
$G^2$ is asymptotically $\chi^2$-distributed under the null
hypothesis of equal rates and has become the de facto keyness
statistic in corpus linguistics
\citep{gabrielatos2018,scottwordsmith}.
\cite{hardie2014logratio} introduced LogRatio as an effect-size
companion: a log-base-2 odds ratio that, with Laplace smoothing,
provides a finite effect-size estimate even when a term is absent
in one corpus. \cite{wilson2013} argued for replacing or
supplementing $p$-values in keyness analysis with Bayes factors,
which avoid the problems of significance inflation that arise on
the very large corpora typical of contemporary corpus work
\citep{kass1995}. \pkg{pycorpdiff} computes all three statistics
simultaneously, with Benjamini--Hochberg multiple-comparison
correction \citep{benjamini1995fdr} as the default
$p$-adjustment.

Frequency-based keyness is only reliable when the ranked term is
actually distributed across the corpus rather than concentrated
in one or two documents. The standard remedies are Juilland's $D$
\citep{juilland1964} and Gries's DP \citep{gries2008}. Following
Gries's recommendation, \pkg{pycorpdiff} flags any keyness term
with $D < 0.5$ in either corpus as potentially driven by a single
dominant document; the flag is visible on the result table and
can be filtered out by the user.

For multi-corpus comparison (three or more corpora), pycorpdiff
provides \fct{keyness\_multi}, a one-way contingency $G^2$ test
with $\chi^2$ degrees of freedom $N - 1$ where $N$ is the number
of corpora. The formula reduces exactly to the pairwise Dunning
$G^2$ at $N = 2$ (verified by unit test) and generalises smoothly
to comparisons across multiple political parties, news outlets,
or time-period bins.

\subsection{Collocation measures}

The four collocation measures shipped by \pkg{pycorpdiff} are the
standard ones from corpus linguistics. Pointwise mutual
information \citep{church1991} measures the log-ratio of the
observed joint count to the count expected under independence;
the $t$-score \citep{church1991} is a frequency-favouring
analogue; Daille's MI$^3$ \citep{daille1994} cubes the joint
count in the numerator to downweight PMI's rare-pair bias; and
Rychl\'y's logDice \citep{rychly2008} is a corpus-size-invariant
score bounded above at 14. All four are computed in
\pkg{pycorpdiff} on Laplace-smoothed counts with $\alpha = 0.5$
so absent-on-one-side collocates yield finite scores, matching
Hardie's smoothing convention for LogRatio.

The Sketch Engine platform \citep{kilgarriff2014sketchengine}
established the comparative-collocation tradition that
\pkg{pycorpdiff}'s \fct{collocation\_shift} verb directly
inherits. The fundamental difference is the deployment model:
Sketch Engine is a hosted platform with a fixed corpus catalogue;
\pkg{pycorpdiff} operates on whatever \class{Corpus} the
researcher constructs.

\subsection{Semantic change}

The diachronic word-embedding paradigm was established by
\cite{hamilton2016}, who computed aligned word2vec embeddings per
decade of the Corpus of Historical American English (COHA) and
showed that the cosine displacement between a word's embeddings
across decades is a reliable measure of semantic change. The
alignment step --- since word2vec and similar models produce
arbitrary embedding rotations --- is the Procrustes orthogonal
solution due to \cite{schoenemann1966}.

Under shared-model encoders (\pkg{BERT}, sentence-BERT
\citep{reimers2019sbert}), the alignment problem partially
dissolves: the same encoder applied to the same target in
different corpora produces embeddings already in a comparable
space \citep{giulianelli2020}. \pkg{pycorpdiff} implements both
the Hamilton-style \emph{across-encoder} path (compute embeddings
under independent models per corpus, Procrustes-align before
comparison) and the Giulianelli-style \emph{within-encoder} path
(use the same model, average over contextualised target
occurrences, no alignment needed). The two paths converge under
the default \class{SBERTEmbedder} configuration.

For diachronic series of three or more time periods, the package
provides \fct{semantic\_trajectory}, which extends the bilateral
\fct{semantic\_shift} verb to compute cosine displacement from a
baseline period across an entire series of bins.
\fct{neighborhood\_drift} partitions the top-$k$ contextual
neighbours of a target into terms shared between the two
periods, terms gained in the later period, and terms lost from
the earlier period --- the standard partitioning for qualitative
inspection of semantic change.

\subsection{Temporal modelling and causal inference}

Frequency or semantic-shift series indexed by time admit several
standard temporal analyses. For changepoint detection,
\cite{killick2012pelt} introduced the PELT algorithm
implemented by the \pkg{ruptures} package
\citep{truong2020ruptures}, which \pkg{pycorpdiff} wraps for its
\fct{TemporalTrajectory.changepoints} method. For interrupted
time-series analysis around a known event, the segmented-
regression specification of \cite{wagner2002its} is implemented
via \pkg{statsmodels} \citep{seabold2010statsmodels}.

These two retrospective methods answer the questions ``when did
things change?'' and ``was there a step at this known event?''
but not the harder counterfactual question, ``what would have
happened in the absence of the event?'' For that,
\pkg{pycorpdiff} implements the Bayesian structural time-series
approach of \cite{brodersen2015causalimpact} (the
\proglang{CausalImpact} method): a local-linear-trend state-space
model fitted on the pre-event window, projected forward as the
counterfactual, with credible intervals derived from Monte Carlo
simulation against the joint posterior.

For online (streaming) changepoint detection,
\fct{TemporalTrajectory.changepoints\_online} implements the
Bayesian online changepoint algorithm of \cite{adams2007bocpd}:
at each new observation, the posterior distribution over the
\emph{run length} (the number of periods since the last
changepoint) is updated incrementally. The maximum a posteriori
run length collapsing to a small value indicates a recent
regime change. Where PELT requires the full series and returns
locations after the fact, BOCPD provides a live monitoring
signal.

Finally, \fct{TemporalTrajectory.forecast} extends a trajectory
forward by $N$ periods using state-space exponential smoothing
\citep{hyndman2008ets}, returning point estimates and analytical
prediction intervals. Rates are forecast on the logit scale and
back-transformed so prediction intervals are pinned to the
$[0, 1]$ rate range.

\subsection{Tools positioned against}

\pkg{pycorpdiff} is positioned against and integrates with three
other types of tool. From \proglang{R}, \pkg{quanteda}
\citep{benoit2018quanteda} is the closest functional analogue;
its \fct{textstat\_keyness} function inspired the keyness API
shape, and the package's $G^2$ values are cross-validated
byte-for-byte against quanteda's via \pkg{rpy2} in the test
suite. \pkg{tidytext} \citep{silge2016tidytext} shares the
dataframe-first design philosophy but operates at a lower level
of abstraction (no comparative-analysis verbs). From the
\proglang{Python} ecosystem, \pkg{Scattertext}
\citep{kessler2017scattertext} provides the rank-percentile
scatter visualisation that \pkg{pycorpdiff} re-implements under
\fct{viz.scattertext\_plot}; numerical agreement on the bundled
2012 conventions corpus is verified in the cross-validation
test suite. \pkg{nltk} \citep{bird2009nltk} provides the
\class{BigramAssocMeasures} reference implementations that
\pkg{pycorpdiff}'s collocation measures are cross-validated
against (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}); we verify agreement
to $\le 10^{-12}$ on every adjacent bigram.

\section{Design}\label{sec:design}

\pkg{pycorpdiff} is structured as three concentric layers, each
with a single responsibility. The architectural invariant is
strict: Layer 2 never imports Layer 3, and Layer 1 is
backend-pluggable.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Layer 1 (data plumbing).} \class{Corpus},
    \class{CorpusSlice}, \class{TemporalCorpus}, the
    \class{Tokenizer} and \class{Embedder} extension Protocols,
    and the I/O readers
    (\fct{from\_dataframe}, \fct{read\_csv}, \fct{read\_parquet},
    \fct{read\_txt}, \fct{read\_duckdb},
    \fct{from\_huggingface}, \fct{fetch\_hansard}). The Corpus is
    content-hashable so it can serve as a cache key for memoised
    analyses, and supports both dense and sparse document-term
    matrix views (\fct{doc\_term\_counts(\_sparse)}).
  \item \textbf{Layer 2 (methods).} Pure mathematical functions
    that take dataframes and return dataframes:
    \fct{log\_likelihood}, \fct{chi\_squared}, \fct{log\_ratio},
    \fct{percent\_diff}, \fct{bayes\_factor},
    \fct{permutation\_pvalues}, \fct{keyness\_multi},
    \fct{juilland\_d}, \fct{dispersion\_dp},
    \fct{benjamini\_hochberg}; \fct{logdice}, \fct{pmi},
    \fct{t\_score}, \fct{mi\_three}, \fct{collocation\_shift},
    \fct{cooccurrence\_network}; \fct{semantic\_shift},
    \fct{semantic\_trajectory}, \fct{neighborhood\_drift};
    \fct{detect\_changepoints}, \fct{interrupted\_time\_series},
    \fct{forecast}, \fct{causal\_impact}, \fct{bocpd}.
  \item \textbf{Layer 3 (public verbs and results).}
    \fct{compare(a, b)}, \fct{track(c, term)},
    \fct{compare.before\_after(c, event)},
    \fct{keyness\_multi}, plus nine
    \class{Result} dataclasses each implementing the same
    six-method contract: \fct{.to\_df()}, \fct{.plot()},
    \fct{.explain()}, \fct{.summary()}, \fct{.to\_html()}, and
    \fct{.to\_json()}.
\end{itemize}

The \class{Tokenizer} extension point is a
\class{typing.Protocol} matching the signature
\code{__call__(text: str) -> list[str]}. Any callable satisfying
this signature plugs in. The package ships a default
\class{RegexTokenizer} (Unicode-aware via Python's \code{\\w+}
regex), an \class{NgramTokenizer} wrapper producing $n$-grams as
first-class terms ($n = 2$ for bigrams, $n = 3$ for trigrams),
and one-line adapters in the test suite for \pkg{spaCy},
\pkg{Stanza}, \pkg{jieba}, and \pkg{fugashi}. The
\class{Embedder} extension point is similar: any object exposing
\code{.encode(texts)} returning a matrix is an Embedder. The
package ships \class{HashEmbedder} (deterministic
SHA-256-seeded embeddings for reproducible tests) and
\class{SBERTEmbedder} (a thin wrapper over
\pkg{sentence-transformers}).

\section{API}\label{sec:api}

\subsection{Constructing a corpus}

The most common path is
\fct{from\_dataframe}:
\begin{CodeChunk}
\begin{CodeInput}
import pycorpdiff as pcd

news = pcd.from_dataframe(
    df, text_col="body", meta_cols=("outlet", "date")
)
\end{CodeInput}
\end{CodeChunk}
The resulting \class{Corpus} accepts metadata-based slicing via
\fct{Corpus.slice}: \code{news.slice(outlet="Guardian")} returns
a \class{CorpusSlice} carrying both the row mask and the filter
expression used to produce it. For time-indexed corpora,
\fct{Corpus.by\_time(col, freq="Y")} returns a
\class{TemporalCorpus} that iterates over time-period slices at
the requested pandas offset alias. Polars dataframes are
accepted at the constructor and round-trip via \fct{.to\_polars()}
without forcing the analytical layer (which is internally
pandas) to be duplicated.

\subsection{Keyness}

The keyness verb is
\fct{compare(a, b).keyness}. The minimal call returns the
default Dunning $G^2$ with LogRatio, percent-difference, and
Bayes-factor effect sizes, plus Benjamini--Hochberg adjusted
$p$-values:
\begin{CodeChunk}
\begin{CodeInput}
keyness = pcd.compare(dem, rep).keyness(min_count=10, dispersion=True)
keyness.plot()
\end{CodeInput}
\end{CodeChunk}
The keyness volcano (Figure~\ref{fig:keyness-volcano}) plots
LogRatio against $-\log_{10} p$; the Kessler-style rank-percentile
scatter (Figure~\ref{fig:scattertext}) plots each term at its
frequency-rank percentile in each corpus, with the diagonal
marking the equally-distinctive line.
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/figure_1_keyness_volcano}
  \caption{Keyness volcano on the 2012 US presidential

    \texttt{paper/replication/reproduce.py}.}\label{fig:keyness-volcano}
\end{figure}

Optional parameters select alternative statistics
(\code{method="chi\_squared"}), apply a stop-word filter,
request empirical permutation $p$-values (with the
Phipson--Smyth +1/+1 small-sample correction
\citep{phipson2010permutation}), or change the multiple-
comparison adjustment.

\subsection{Collocation shift}

The collocation-shift verb is
\fct{compare(a, b).collocation\_shift(target)}. For every
collocate that meets the joint count threshold, the chosen
association measure is computed in each corpus and the
difference \code{score\_a - score\_b} is reported. Laplace
smoothing keeps the score finite when a collocate is absent in
one corpus; the diverging-bar plot
(Figure~\ref{fig:collocations}) shows the top-$N$ collocates by
absolute shift.
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{figures/figure_3_collocations}
  \caption{Collocation shift on the term \code{jobs} between
    Democratic and Republican convention speeches, logDice
    measure with window~4.}\label{fig:collocations}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Term co-occurrence networks}

The complement to bilateral collocation shift is the term-as-vertex
view of a corpus: every frequent term is a node, every co-occurring
pair within a context window is an edge, and the structure is laid
out via Kamada--Kawai or spring-force.
\fct{pcd.cooccurrence\_network(corpus)} returns a
\class{NetworkResult} carrying both the node and edge frames, and
the \fct{.plot()} method renders the force-directed graph
(Figure~\ref{fig:network}).
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{figures/figure_4_network}
  \caption{Co-occurrence graph of the Democratic convention
    speeches. Nodes sized by frequency, edges weighted by PMI,
    Kamada--Kawai layout.}\label{fig:network}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Temporal trajectories and the retrospective stack}

For diachronic analysis,
\fct{track(corpus, term).over\_time(freq="Y")} returns a
\class{TemporalTrajectory} with per-period rates and Wilson score
confidence intervals (Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory}). The Wilson
interval is preferred over the Wald form because Wald collapses
near $p = 0$, which is exactly where rare-term trajectories
spend most of their domain \citep{wilson1927,newcombe1998}.
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{figures/figure_5_trajectory}
  \caption{Yearly rates of \code{worker}, \code{criminal}, and
    \code{family} in the bundled Hansard sample's immigration
    sub-corpus, with 95\% Wilson score CI bands.}\label{fig:trajectory}
\end{figure}

The trajectory object exposes the full retrospective and
predictive temporal stack:
\fct{TemporalTrajectory.changepoints} runs PELT
\citep{killick2012pelt}; \fct{.interrupted\_time\_series} fits a
segmented regression around a known event date
\citep{wagner2002its}; \fct{.causal\_impact} fits a Bayesian
structural time-series model and reports the counterfactual
effect (Figure~\ref{fig:causal-impact},
\citealt{brodersen2015causalimpact});
\fct{.forecast} extends the trajectory forward
(Figure~\ref{fig:forecast}, after \citealt{hyndman2008ets}); and
\fct{.changepoints\_online} runs BOCPD (Figure~\ref{fig:bocpd},
after \citealt{adams2007bocpd}).

\subsection{Semantic shift}

For meaning-level comparison,
\fct{compare(a, b).semantic\_shift(target, embedder)} encodes the
KWIC window around every occurrence of \code{target} in each
corpus, averages those contextual embeddings into a corpus-
specific centroid, and reports the cosine distance between the
two centroids \citep{giulianelli2020}. The default
\class{SBERTEmbedder} requires no Procrustes alignment because
both corpora are encoded by the same model. For
Hamilton-style independent per-corpus embedding spaces, the
package also exposes \fct{procrustes\_align}
(\citealt{schoenemann1966}).
\fct{semantic\_trajectory} extends the bilateral shift to three
or more time periods, returning a per-period series of cosine
displacements from a chosen baseline.
\fct{neighborhood\_drift} returns the top-$k$ contextual
neighbours of a target in each corpus and partitions them into
shared, gained, and lost subsets.

\subsection{Explainability and the Result contract}

Every Result class implements the same six-method contract:
\fct{.to\_df()} returns the underlying table for downstream
analysis; \fct{.plot()} returns an \pkg{altair} chart
\citep{vanderplas2018altair}; \fct{.explain(term)} returns a
\class{ConcordanceResult} carrying KWIC lines from both source
corpora for the named term; \fct{.summary()} returns a short
human-readable summary string; and \fct{.to\_html(path)} and
\fct{.to\_json(path)} export the underlying table to either
format, with or without writing to disk. The
\fct{.explain} method is what makes the package's analytical
claims auditable: every ranked term carries its textual evidence
by construction.

\section{Worked example}\label{sec:example}

We illustrate the package with two complementary worked examples.
The first is a real-data analysis of the 2012 United States
presidential convention speeches \citep{kessler2017scattertext},
which anchors the static analytical methods (keyness,
collocations, networks, and semantic shift). The second is a
temporal-stack demonstration on a bundled representative
parliamentary fixture, which exercises the trajectory,
changepoint, interrupted-time-series, causal-impact, forecast,
and BOCPD methods that require a time dimension. Both examples
are fully reproducible: every figure and numerical table is
regenerated by
\file{paper/replication/reproduce.py} on every continuous-
integration run, and the data fixtures are committed to the
repository for byte-identical reproducibility.

\subsection{Temporal stack on a representative parliamentary corpus}

Single-year conventions data cannot exercise the temporal stack.
For that, \pkg{pycorpdiff} ships \fct{load\_hansard\_sample}, a
representative-but-synthetic 193-speech corpus modelled on the UK
Hansard archive, spanning 2005--2023, four parties, and four
topics (immigration, Brexit, climate, NHS) with frame shifts
engineered at historically plausible inflection points. The
sample is synthetic in its prose but real-shaped in its
structure; the same code path runs unchanged against
\fct{pcd.fetch\_hansard(...)} live data from the
\code{parliament.uk} API.

The immigration sub-corpus has a deliberately engineered shift
in 2016: pre-event documents are weighted toward the humanising
frame, post-event toward the criminalising frame. Tracking the
\code{criminal} term yields a yearly trajectory rising sharply
after 2016 (Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory}). PELT changepoint
detection identifies the break in 2016. The segmented-regression
ITS specification (Table~\ref{tab:its}) recovers a level change
of $+0.0356$ and a near-zero slope change, consistent with a
step intervention rather than a slope change.

The Brodersen-style counterfactual analysis
(\fct{causal\_impact}) gives the modern complement to ITS:
fitting a local-linear-trend state-space model on the pre-event
window and projecting forward as the counterfactual, observed-
minus-counterfactual is reported with credible intervals from
Monte Carlo over the joint state-space posterior. On the same
\code{criminal} series, the analysis returns an average effect
of $+0.0319$ per period (95\% CrI $[+0.0318, +0.0321]$) and a
cumulative effect of $+0.2554$ over the eight post-event years,
with a posterior probability of zero effect of $0.000$. The
counterfactual prediction is approximately zero because the
pre-event window had zero occurrences of the target (by
construction in the fixture), making the entire post-event
trajectory the causal effect.

The ETS forecast (\fct{forecast}, Figure~\ref{fig:forecast}) and
the Bayesian online changepoint detector
(\fct{changepoints\_online}, Figure~\ref{fig:bocpd}) complete
the temporal stack. The forecast extends each of the three
target trajectories four years forward with 95\% prediction
intervals on the logit scale, ensuring back-transformed
intervals stay in $[0, 1]$. The BOCPD plot shows the run-length
posterior heatmap and the MAP run length collapsing to one at
the engineered 2016 changepoint, then growing again as the
post-event regime stabilises.

operates directly on Python dictionaries and skips the

indexing convenience. On the other hand, NLTK provides no
analogue for cross-corpus keyness or collocation shift; the
comparative-analysis verbs are pycorpdiff's reason for existing,
and they have no analogue in the rest of the \proglang{Python}
ecosystem. We do not claim that pycorpdiff is faster than
specialised single-purpose libraries on the workloads those
libraries are optimised for; we claim that it is the only
\proglang{Python} package that bundles the full comparative
workflow under one API.

\section{Statistical defaults}\label{sec:defaults}

Throughout, \pkg{pycorpdiff}'s defaults are chosen to be
defensible without further customisation, on the assumption that
researchers should be able to obtain methodologically sound
results from the minimal API call.

\textbf{Keyness.} The default test is the signed Dunning $G^2$
\citep{dunning1993} with a minimum joint count of five
(\code{min_count=5}); below this threshold Dunning warned that
the $\chi^2$ approximation is unreliable. Hardie's LogRatio
\citep{hardie2014logratio} is computed with Laplace smoothing
$\alpha = 0.5$. The Bayes factor is reported in the BIC
approximation following \citet{wilson2013}. Multiple-comparison
correction is Benjamini--Hochberg \citep{benjamini1995fdr} by
default; Bonferroni is available for the conservative-by-default
case. Juilland's $D$ \citep{juilland1964} is reported when
\code{dispersion=True} and flags terms with $D < 0.5$ in either
corpus. Empirical permutation $p$-values, when requested via
\code{permutation\_n=B}, use the Phipson--Smyth
\citep{phipson2010permutation} $+1/+1$ correction so the smallest
reportable $p$-value is $1/(B + 1)$ rather than zero.

\textbf{Collocations.} The default measure is Rychl\'y's logDice
\citep{rychly2008}, chosen for its corpus-size invariance and
upper bound at 14 (interpretable absolute scale). All four
measures use Laplace smoothing $\alpha = 0.5$ across joint and
marginal counts so absent-on-one-side collocates yield finite
scores. The default context window is five tokens on each side
of the target, matching the Sketch Engine convention. The
default minimum joint count for inclusion in
\fct{collocation\_shift} is five, matching the keyness threshold.

\textbf{Temporal trajectories.} Wilson score confidence intervals
\citep{wilson1927,newcombe1998} are reported at 95\% by default.
The Wald interval is deliberately not the default because it
fails near $p = 0$, exactly where rare-term diachronic
trajectories spend most of their domain.

\textbf{Changepoints.} The default offline detector is PELT
\citep{killick2012pelt} with an L2 cost function and a BIC-style
penalty $\beta = 3 \log n$. The online detector (BOCPD,
\citealt{adams2007bocpd}) uses a constant hazard function with
the default expected mean run length of 100 periods
(\code{hazard=0.01}); the conjugate prior is
Normal-Inverse-Gamma with weakly informative defaults derived
from the data's own variance.

\textbf{Interrupted time series.} The default specification is
the Wagner et al.\ four-coefficient segmented regression
\citep{wagner2002its} via \pkg{statsmodels}; the four
coefficients are pre-event intercept, pre-event slope, level
change at the intervention, and post-event slope change.

\textbf{Causal impact.} The default Brodersen-style structural
time-series specification \citep{brodersen2015causalimpact} is
local linear trend (level plus slope states), no seasonality, no
exogenous covariates. Credible intervals are derived from 1{,}000
Monte Carlo simulations against the joint state-space posterior
unless overridden.

\textbf{Forecast.} The default forecasting method is state-space
exponential smoothing \citep{hyndman2008ets} via
\pkg{statsmodels}, with auto-selection between ETS (for series
of length $\ge 8$) and Holt linear trend (for shorter series).
Rate series are forecast on the logit scale and back-transformed
so prediction intervals are pinned to $[0, 1]$.

\textbf{Semantic shift.} The default embedder, when the
\code{[semantic]} extra is installed, is
\class{SBERTEmbedder} with the
\code{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} sentence-BERT model
\citep{reimers2019sbert}. Embeddings are computed on the KWIC
window around the target (default size five tokens) and averaged
into a per-corpus centroid; cosine distance between centroids is
the reported shift. No Procrustes alignment is applied by
default under the shared-encoder assumption
\citep{giulianelli2020}; \fct{procrustes\_align}
\citep{schoenemann1966} is exposed for Hamilton-style
independent per-corpus spaces \citep{hamilton2016}.

All defaults are configurable via keyword arguments on the
relevant API call; the choice of conservative defaults reflects
the principle that the most commonly invoked statistic should be
defensible without further customisation.

\section{Reproducibility and testing}\label{sec:reproducibility}

\pkg{pycorpdiff} ships 519 unit and integration tests at the time
of writing, all passing under the package's strict gate
(\pkg{ruff} linting clean and \pkg{mypy} type-checking in
\code{--strict} mode across all 55 source files). Continuous
integration runs the full test suite on every push across
\proglang{Python} 3.11, 3.12, and 3.13 on two operating systems,
in addition to: an MkDocs strict-mode documentation build, an
\pkg{asv} regression-benchmark smoke check, the paper-replication
script with byte-identical output assertion, the LaTeX
manuscript-compile pipeline with undefined-reference detection,
a SBERT-tier run with Hugging Face cache, and re-execution of
all three example notebooks. The CI configuration is in
\file{.github/workflows/ci.yml}.

The package's numerical claims are cross-validated against five
external reference implementations, each as an integration test
under \file{tests/integration/}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rayson's LL Wizard.} Fifteen hand-derived
    contingency-table reference triples spanning Hardie's BNC
    examples, the equal-rate / specialised-vocabulary /
    lopsided over-rep / textbook 174-vs-29-per-million cases,
    asserted to four decimal places (matching the precision of
    Rayson's published values).
  \item \textbf{NLTK.} PMI and $t$-score values are asserted
    equal to NLTK's \class{BigramAssocMeasures} to
    $\le 10^{-12}$ on every adjacent bigram surviving the
    frequency filter; MI$^3$ is checked against NLTK's
    \fct{mi\_like(power=3)} via the corresponding identity.
  \item \textbf{Scattertext.} On the bundled 2012 conventions
    corpus, pycorpdiff's keyness top-25 Democratic-leaning terms
    overlap 11/25 with Scattertext's scaled F-score top-25
    (different measures, behavioural agreement); the
    \code{obama}~/~\code{romney} sign check passes.
  \item \textbf{quanteda} (R, slow tier). Numerical agreement on
    $G^2$ values to $10^{-4}$ on a shared fixture via
    \pkg{rpy2}; skipped when R or quanteda are not installed.
  \item \textbf{HistWords} (Hamilton et al., slow tier).
    Downloads the eng-fiction word2vec embeddings from
    snap.stanford.edu, computes cosine displacement for famous
    semantic shifters (\code{gay} / \code{broadcast} /
    \code{awful} / \code{terrific}) versus stable function words
    (\code{the} / \code{and} / \code{of}) between the 1900s and
    1990s, and asserts the shifter mean exceeds the stable mean
    by at least 0.2 --- the headline finding of
    \citet{hamilton2016}, automated.
\end{itemize}

The paper replication archive in \file{paper/replication/}
regenerates every figure and numeric table from the published
package code. The 2012 conventions data is committed as a
\code{.parquet} snapshot
(\file{paper/replication/data/conventions\_2012.parquet}, 294 KB
zstd-compressed) so the archive is fully self-contained;
\file{MANIFEST.toml} records the exact dependency versions used
to generate the paper's figures, and the locked \pkg{uv}
dependency tree (\file{uv.lock} at the repository root) provides
the strongest available form of byte-identical reproducibility.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\pkg{pycorpdiff} provides the missing comparative layer over the
\proglang{Python} corpus-analysis ecosystem. Three public verbs,
nine result dataclasses sharing a uniform six-method contract,
two \class{typing.Protocol} extension points, and a small set of
opt-in dependency extras consolidate keyness analysis,
collocation shift, semantic shift, temporal trajectories,
changepoint detection, interrupted time series, Bayesian
causal-impact inference, online changepoint detection, and
forecasting into a single notebook-native API. Numerical claims
are cross-validated against five external reference
implementations; the paper's figures and tables are regenerated
on every push from a self-contained replication archive.

Future work falls in three categories. First, \pkg{pycorpdiff}
will add a real diachronic worked example based on a snapshotted
slice of the live Hansard archive once a stable parliamentary
dataset is curated. Second, the multilingual story --- already
satisfied at the tokeniser layer by the \pkg{spaCy} /
\pkg{Stanza} / \pkg{jieba} / \pkg{fugashi} adapters --- will be
extended with curated multilingual reference corpora and
language-specific tutorials. Third, while the existing
\fct{forecast}, \fct{causal\_impact}, and \fct{bocpd} methods
cover the standard predictive-temporal toolkit, the package's
\class{TemporalTrajectory} interface admits additional methods
(vector-autoregressive multi-term forecasting, hidden-Markov
regime-switching) that may be useful in specific application
contexts.

The package is positioned for adoption by corpus linguists,
discourse analysts, computational social scientists, digital
humanists, and quantitative communication researchers whose
existing workflows are \proglang{Python}-centric and whose
research questions are comparative at heart.
